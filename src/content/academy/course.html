Module 0 · Week 1

Professional Requirements & Responsibilities

Before you learn a single line of a tax return, two things have to be true: you are legally allowed to be paid for this work, and you understand what happens when it goes wrong.

By the end you will
  • Have your own PTIN
  • Know what you may and may not do as a paid preparer
  • Be able to say, in dollars, what one careless return costs
  • Know the rules for handling client information

Your PTIN — do this first

A PTIN is a Preparer Tax Identification Number. The IRS issues it to you, personally. The rule is simple and there is no way around it: anyone who prepares, or helps prepare, a federal tax return for money must have one.

Not the firm — you. It goes on every return you touch.

Your first assignment

Register at the IRS Tax Professional portal. It takes about fifteen minutes. Check the current fee on the portal before you start — it has changed before. Send your confirmation to your trainer before Friday of Week 1.

Two things people get wrong. Don't use the paper form — it takes six weeks and you do not have six weeks. And PTINs run by calendar year: you are preparing returns in January 2027, so you need a 2027 PTIN.

The rules you work under

Paid preparers are governed by a Treasury rulebook called Circular 230. You don't need to memorise it. You do need to live by five things:

  1. Due diligence is constant. It isn't a form you fill in at the end. It's the questions you ask all the way through.
  2. You cannot sign a return you know is wrong. Not "probably fine". Not "the client insisted".
  3. Your fee can never be a share of the refund. If a client offers you a cut, the answer is no.
  4. Keep your records — three years, and we'll show you where.
  5. Don't overstate what you are. You are a tax preparer, not an Enrolled Agent or a CPA. You cannot represent someone in an audit, and you must never imply you can.

What one careless return actually costs

This is the part people skim. Please don't.

There is a penalty — IRC §6695(g) — for failing to do your due diligence. Not for getting the tax wrong: for not doing the work of checking. And it does not apply once per return. It applies once per credit:

On the same returnPenalties
Earned Income Tax Creditone
Child Tax Credit familyone
American Opportunity Creditone
Head of Household statusone
One return, all four4 ×
Look it up. Don't recall it.

The dollar amount is adjusted for inflation every year. Whatever figure you memorise today will be out of date before you use it — and a preparer who confidently quotes last year's number is exactly the preparer this module is trying not to produce.

Check it on IRS.gov/Form8867 each October and write it on your figures sheet. What you keep is the shape: four times, per return, plus the firm.

And it doesn't stop with you

The IRS can assess the firm for your failures, and can suspend the firm from e-filing altogether. One preparer being careless can shut down everyone's season.

That is why the December exam is real, and why nobody meets a client before passing it. It isn't about doubting you. It's that the stakes are genuinely this high.

Client information

You will be handling Social Security numbers, income, addresses and children's names. Treat every file like it's your own.

  • Never on personal email, personal cloud storage, or an unlocked phone
  • Working from home? Only on the device the firm has approved, and lock it when you step away
  • Never discuss a client's return where anyone can overhear — including family
  • Lost a device, or sent a file to the wrong person? Tell your trainer within the hour. You will not be in trouble for reporting it fast. You will be for hiding it.

You'll read and sign the firm's security plan before you see a real client file.

Knowledge check

Five questions. 70% to pass — you can retake this.

1. A client offers you 10% of their refund as your fee. What do you do?

Contingent fees based on refund size are prohibited.

2. You're preparing a return in January 2027. Which year's PTIN do you need?

PTINs run on the calendar year you are working in, not the tax year of the return.

3. Due diligence failed on EITC, the Child Tax Credit and Head of Household on one return. What's the exposure?

One per failed credit, and Head of Household counts as one of the four. Three failures, three penalties, and the firm carries exposure as well. Look up the current amount rather than recalling it.

4. You only help with part of a return and don't sign it. Do you need a PTIN?

Preparing or assisting in preparing for compensation requires a PTIN.

5. You email a client's return from your personal Gmail because the office system is slow. What happened?

Client data never travels on personal accounts. Report it straight away.
Module 1 · Weeks 1–2

The 1040, End to End

Every return you ever prepare is this one form. Learn its shape now and everything afterwards has somewhere to sit.

By the end you will
  • Walk a client through a 1040 and explain what each part does
  • Read a W-2 and know where every figure goes
  • Decide whether someone has to file — and whether they should anyway
  • Complete a simple return on your own

A tax return is a story in five movements

Don't memorise line numbers. Learn the shape:

  1. Who you are — name, Social Security number, filing status, who else is in the household
  2. What came in — wages, self-employment, everything else
  3. What comes off — adjustments, then the standard deduction (or itemised, which is rarer than you'd think)
  4. What's owed — the tax, then credits that reduce it
  5. What's already been paid — withholding and refundable credits. The difference is the refund, or the bill.

When you meet something new later — a Schedule C, the EITC, a Detroit return — ask which movement it belongs to. It always belongs to one of them.

The W-2

Most of your clients have one, and their whole return turns on it. The boxes that matter most:

Why Box 1 and Box 3 differ

Money put into a 401(k) comes out of Box 1 but not out of Box 3 — you don't pay income tax on it now, but you do pay Social Security tax. Health premiums taken pre-tax reduce both.

A client who asks "why does it say I earned two different amounts?" is asking about this. Now you can answer.

Who has to file — and who should anyway

Whether someone is required to file depends mostly on whether their income is above the standard deduction for their status.

Filing statusStandard deduction · TY2026
Single / Married filing separately$16,100
Married filing jointly$32,200

The more important point

Plenty of people who don't have to file absolutely should.

Someone who earned $9,400 and has two children isn't required to file. They may also be owed several thousand dollars, because refundable credits and withheld tax only come back if a return is filed.

You will meet this client. Telling them they don't have to file, and leaving it there, is the wrong answer.

Try it

A client's W-2 shows Box 1 of $38,000 and Box 3 of $41,500. She wants to know which one is her "real" salary, and why they're different. What do you tell her?

Answer

Both are real — they measure different things. Her gross pay was $41,500. She put $3,500 into a retirement plan, which comes out before income tax is calculated, so her taxable wage is $38,000. Social Security tax is still charged on the full amount, which is why Box 3 didn't drop.

Short version for the client: "You earned $41,500. You saved $3,500 of it into your retirement plan before tax, so you're only taxed on $38,000."

Knowledge check

Five questions. 70% to pass.

1. Which W-2 boxes tell you a Detroit city return may be needed?

18–20 are the local boxes. 15–17 are state.

2. A client earned $9,400 and has two children. What do you tell her?

Not required, but refundable credits are only paid out if a return is filed.

3. What is the TY2026 standard deduction for Head of Household?

$24,150 — between Single and Married filing jointly.

4. Box 1 is lower than Box 3. Give the most common reason.

401(k) contributions reduce taxable wages but not Social Security wages.

5. Where does a refundable credit sit in the five movements?

Refundable credits are treated like payments, which is why they can produce a refund even when no tax was owed.
Module 2 · Week 3

Filing Status

One decision, made in the first two minutes of the interview, that changes more money than almost anything else on the return. It is also something the IRS penalises you for getting wrong.

By the end you will
  • Apply the three Head of Household tests to a real family
  • Work out whether a separated client is "considered unmarried"
  • Know who counts as a qualifying person — and who doesn't
  • Understand why this is a due-diligence item

The rule that settles a third of all questions

Marital status is decided on 31 December. Not on average over the year. Not when they separated.

Divorced on 30 December? Unmarried for the entire year. Married on 30 December? Married for the entire year. Ask the date, every time.

Head of Household — three tests, all required

  1. Unmarried on 31 December — or "considered unmarried", which we'll come to
  2. Paid more than half the cost of keeping up the home for the year
  3. A qualifying person lived with them for more than half the year

All three. Not two out of three.

What counts as "cost of keeping up a home"

Counts: rent or mortgage interest, property taxes, home insurance, utilities, repairs, and food eaten in the home.

Doesn't count: clothing, education, medical care, holidays, or the value of the client's own work around the house.

One exception worth remembering: a dependent parent is a qualifying person even if they don't live with your client — as long as your client pays more than half the cost of the parent's home. A parent in a care home can still make someone Head of Household.

"Considered unmarried"

A client who is still married can sometimes still file Head of Household — but every one of these must be true:

  • They file a separate return
  • They paid more than half the cost of the home
  • Their spouse did not live in the home at any point during the last six months of the year
  • The home was their child's main home for more than half the year
  • They can claim that child as a dependant
This is where it's won or lost

The six-month rule catches people out constantly. A spouse who moved out in September was in the home in July. That fails. The client is Married Filing Separately — no matter what they were told last year, and no matter how certain they are.

So ask for the date he or she left. And write it down.

Why this is a due-diligence item

Head of Household is covered by Form 8867, the same form as the credits. That means the due-diligence penalty applies here too — one more of the four.

"The client told me she was head of household" is not due diligence. Asking who lived in the home, for how many months, and who paid the bills — and recording the answers — is.

Try it · 1 of 4

Denise lived with her boyfriend for all of 2026. She paid the entire rent and every utility bill; he contributed nothing. They have no children. He is not related to her. What's her filing status?

Single

She passes test 2 easily — she paid everything. But test 3 fails. A qualifying person for Head of Household has to be a relative. An unrelated partner can sometimes be claimed as a dependant, but someone who qualifies only by living in the home never makes your client Head of Household.

The trap: seeing "supported another adult, paid all the bills" and reaching for HOH. Paying is test 2. Test 3 is a different question entirely.

Try it · 2 of 4

Marcus's divorce was finalised on 18 December 2026. His nine-year-old daughter lived with him all year and he paid every bill in the house.

Head of Household

Status is set on 31 December, so Marcus is unmarried for the whole year — even though he was married for eleven and a half months of it. He paid more than half the cost, and his daughter lived with him more than half the year. All three tests met.

The trap: treating him as married because he was married for most of the year.

Try it · 3 of 4

Angela's husband moved out on 12 September 2026 and hasn't been back. Their son lived with Angela all year and she pays the mortgage. She's certain she qualifies as Head of Household because "he's gone".

Married filing separately

Angela was still married on 31 December, so she needs "considered unmarried". Her husband lived in the home during the last six months of the year — he was there through July, August and half of September. That test fails, so Head of Household isn't available.

Her options are Married Filing Separately, or Married Filing Jointly if her husband will sign.

The trap: the client's certainty. She isn't lying — she's just answering a different question. Ask for the date.

Try it · 4 of 4

Ruth is 58, unmarried, and has raised her seven-year-old grandson since 2023. He lives with her full time and she supports him entirely.

Head of Household

A grandchild is a descendant of Ruth's child, so the relationship works. He lived with her all year and she paid more than half the cost of the home. All three tests met.

More importantly: Ruth should also be screened for the EITC and the Child Tax Credit. Grandparents raising grandchildren are routinely entitled to far more than they claim, usually because nobody asked.

Knowledge check

Six questions. 70% to pass.

1. On what date is marital status determined?

The last day of the tax year, always.

2. Which qualifying person does not have to live with the taxpayer?

A dependent parent — provided the client pays more than half the cost of the parent's home.

3. A client's spouse moved out in July. Can she be "considered unmarried"?

The spouse must not have lived there at any point in the last six months. July is inside that window.

4. Is food eaten in the home part of the cost of keeping up a home?

Food eaten in the home counts. Clothing, education and medical care do not.

5. Does Form 8867 apply to Head of Household?

HOH is a due-diligence item in its own right.

6. An unrelated partner lives with your client, who supports them entirely. Does that make your client Head of Household?

Someone who qualifies only by living in the home never supports HOH status.
Module 3 · Weeks 4–5 · the big one

The Earned Income Tax Credit

The largest credit most of our clients will ever claim — up to $8,231. It's also the most audited item on the return and the one that carries the penalty. Take your time with this module.

By the end you will
  • Apply the four qualifying child tests without looking them up
  • Settle a dispute when two people claim the same child
  • Complete Form 8867 and meet all four due-diligence requirements
  • Write notes that will still defend the return in two years

What "earned" means

Earned income is money someone worked for: wages, salary, tips, and net profit from self-employment.

It is not: unemployment compensation, Social Security, child support, alimony, pensions, or investment income.

A client whose entire year was unemployment has no EITC. That's a hard conversation, and it's far better had at your desk than in a letter from the IRS eight months later.

Three things that kill the credit outright
  • Anyone on the return without a valid Social Security number
  • Investment income over $12,200 — over by a single dollar and the whole credit is gone. There's no tapering.
  • The client is themselves someone else's qualifying child

The four tests — learn these as R-A-R-J

  1. Relationship. Son, daughter, stepchild, foster child, brother, sister, half- or step-sibling — or a descendant of any of them. So grandchildren, nieces and nephews all count. A cousin does not.
  2. Age. Under 19 at year end and younger than your client; or under 24, a full-time student, and younger than your client; or any age if permanently and totally disabled.
  3. Residency. Lived with your client in the United States for more than half the year. Time away at school, in hospital, or on military service still counts as living at home.
  4. Joint return. The child didn't file a joint return — unless it was purely to claim back withheld tax.

When two people claim the same child

It happens constantly. The rules decide, in this order:

  1. A parent beats a non-parent
  2. Between two parents — whoever the child lived with for more nights
  3. If the nights are equal — the parent with the higher income
  4. If neither is a parent — the higher income, provided it beats any parent who could have claimed

In practice, if the other parent has already filed, your client's return will reject. That's a conversation, not a software problem, and we'll give you the words for it in Module 7.

Your four due-diligence duties

  1. Complete and file Form 8867 with the return
  2. Compute the credit using the worksheets — and keep them
  3. Knowledge. You may not rely on information you know, or should know, is wrong, inconsistent or incomplete. Where something doesn't add up you must ask, and write down what you asked and what they said
  4. Keep the records for three years
Number 3 is where preparers actually get caught

The test is simple: does this story hold together?

Someone reporting $13,800 of cash cleaning income with no expenses at all — no supplies, no petrol — landing exactly where the credit peaks, is a story that needs a question asked of it. Not because they're dishonest. Because you're required to ask.

Notes that hold up

Two years from now you will not remember this client. Your note has to. Write it like this:

"14 Feb — asked how many months D. lived with client in 2026. Client said Jan–Aug. Produced school enrolment letter showing client's address. Copy kept."

Fifteen seconds to write. Settles the whole question later.

Try it · 1 of 4

Keisha and Terrance aren't married and share custody of their daughter. In 2026 she stayed with Keisha for seven months and Terrance for five. Both want to claim her. Terrance earns $48,000; Keisha earns $22,000. Who claims?

Keisha

Both are parents, so we go to the second rule: nights. The daughter lived with Keisha longer, so Keisha claims her. Income is irrelevant here — it only comes into play if the nights are equal.

The trap: jumping to income because it's the easier number to get hold of. Ask about nights first.

Try it · 2 of 4

Carl's son turned 19 in March 2026 and lived at home all year, working part time for $6,200. Can Carl claim him for EITC?

It depends entirely on one question you must ask

Was he a full-time student for at least five months of the year?

If yes — he's under 24, a student, and younger than Carl, so the age test passes and he can be a qualifying child.

If no — he's 19 and not a student. The age test fails. He is not a qualifying child for EITC.

The trap: not asking at all. This single question is worth thousands of dollars. Ask it, record the answer, and ask to see the enrolment letter.

Try it · 3 of 4

Yolanda's nephew came to live with her in August 2026 when his mother became unwell, and was still there at the end of the year.

No EITC on the nephew for 2026

A nephew passes the relationship test — he's a descendant of Yolanda's sibling. But August to December is five months, and the test is more than half the year. Residency fails.

He may well qualify next year. This year he doesn't.

The trap: rounding months up to get over the line. More than half the year means more than six months. Write down the month he arrived.

Try it · 4 of 4

A new client reports $13,800 of cash income from house cleaning, no expenses at all, and two qualifying children. The figure lands right where the credit peaks. She has no records of any kind. What do you do?

Ask, document — and be ready to decline

A cleaning business with zero expenses isn't consistent with how that work is actually done. There are always supplies, and usually travel.

Your duty is to make reasonable inquiries and record them: How many clients? How often? What do you buy? How do you get there? How did you arrive at that figure?

If the answers are credible, prepare the return and keep the notes. If they aren't, you don't sign it. That is an uncomfortable thing to do and it is the job.

This exact pattern is the most common EITC penalty case there is. If you remember one scenario from this module, make it this one.

Knowledge check

Seven questions. 70% to pass.

1. What do the four qualifying child tests stand for?

R-A-R-J.

2. Is unemployment compensation earned income for EITC?

Taxable, but not earned. No EITC comes from it.

3. Two parents; the child lived with each exactly the same number of nights. Who claims?

Nights first; higher income only as the tie-break. The credit is never split.

4. A client's investment income is $12,240. What happens to the EITC?

Over the $12,200 limit by any amount and the whole credit goes.

5. A child lived with the taxpayer from 1 February to 31 December. Does residency pass?

The test is more than half the year, not the whole year.

6. How long must due-diligence records be kept?

Three years.

7. A client's figures look inconsistent but she insists they're right. What does the knowledge requirement oblige you to do?

Ask, document, and decline to sign if the answers aren't credible.
Module 4 · Week 6

Side Hustles and Schedule C

Rideshare, hair, cleaning, resale, contracting. A large and growing share of our clients, and the place where the EITC and self-employment tax collide.

By the end you will
  • Know when income belongs on a Schedule C
  • Identify real business expenses — and turn down the rest
  • Explain self-employment tax to someone who's never paid it
  • Tell a business from a hobby

The form the money arrived on doesn't decide anything

1099-NEC, 1099-K, an app payout, a personal cheque, cash in an envelope — if the work was a business, it all lands on a Schedule C.

Cash income is reportable whether or not anyone issued a form. Clients often genuinely don't know this. Say it plainly and without judgement.

Expenses

The standard is ordinary and necessary. For our clients that usually means supplies, mileage, phone, a share of home costs, contract labour, and licences.

Mileage is the big one and the one that gets challenged. The client needs a log. Commuting — home to the first job of the day — is not deductible.

When a client has no records at all, reconstruct what can honestly be reconstructed, write down how you did it, and be straight about what can't be claimed.

Self-employment tax

Employees have Social Security and Medicare taken out, and the employer pays a matching amount. Someone self-employed pays both halves.

This is why a client who cleared $9,000 from a side hustle and expected a refund can end up owing. Half of it comes back as an adjustment, but the shock is real. Warn them, and start the conversation about estimated payments for next year.

Where this module and the EITC meet

Net self-employment profit is earned income for the EITC. Which means claiming fewer expenses can make a client's refund bigger.

Clients work this out. Some will ask you directly to leave expenses off.

The answer is no. You report the expenses that exist. Doing otherwise is a false return, it's a due-diligence failure, and it is exactly what the IRS looks for.

Try it

A rideshare client nets $8,400 and has 6,100 logged business miles. He notices that if he leaves the mileage off, his EITC goes up by more than the tax the extra profit costs him. He asks you to do that. What do you say?

No — and here's how to say it

"I can't leave off expenses you actually had. The return has to show what really happened. If the IRS looks at this — and they do look at exactly this — it would be treated as a false return, and that lands on both of us."

Then give him something real: he's entitled to those miles, and you should make sure his log is good enough to survive a challenge.

The trap: a client who's polite, plausible and persistent. The answer doesn't change.

Knowledge check

Five questions. 70% to pass.

1. A client was paid $2,000 cash and no form was issued. Is it reportable?

Income is reportable regardless of paperwork.

2. Is the drive from home to the first job of the day deductible?

Commuting is never deductible.

3. Why does a self-employed client pay more payroll-type tax than an employee on the same income?

No employer to pay the other half.

4. A client asks you to omit expenses so their EITC is larger. What do you do?

Report actual expenses. Omitting them is a false return.

5. Net profit from self-employment — is it earned income for EITC?

Yes — which is exactly why the expense question matters so much.

Special Situations & the New Deductions

Veterans, retirees and the unemployed — plus a brand-new set of deductions that nobody has filed before. Our clients are unusually likely to qualify for them.

By the end you will
  • Know which veterans' and retirement benefits are taxable
  • Handle unemployment income correctly
  • Screen every client for the new Schedule 1-A deductions

Veterans' benefits

BenefitTaxable?
VA disability compensationNo — don't report it
VA pensionNo
GI Bill education benefitsNo
Military retirement payYes

Clients get this wrong in both directions — some report benefits they shouldn't, others leave off retirement pay they should. Ask specifically which they receive.

Social Security and unemployment

Social Security is taxable on a sliding scale depending on other income — anywhere from none of it up to a maximum of 85%. A retiree who takes a large withdrawal from an IRA can accidentally make their Social Security taxable, and gets surprised twice.

Unemployment is fully taxable, arrives on a 1099-G, and withholding is optional — which most people decline. So a client who was unemployed often owes. And remember from Module 3: it isn't earned income, so it generates no EITC.

Schedule 1-A — new this year

A recent tax law created four new deductions, claimed on a new form. No preparer anywhere has years of experience with these, which means you start level with everyone else.

Four things to remember
  • Tips must have been reported to qualify. Unreported cash tips don't count.

  • Ask every single client. People don't volunteer information they don't know is worth money.

Module 6 · Week 8

Michigan & Detroit Returns

A perfect federal return is half a job. Almost every client who walks through our door also needs a Michigan return, and most need a Detroit one.

By the end you will
  • Prepare an MI-1040 from a finished federal return
  • Spot and claim the Homestead Property Tax Credit
  • Choose correctly between Detroit forms 5118, 5119 and 5120

The Michigan return

Michigan starts from the federal adjusted gross income, applies its own additions and subtractions, and taxes the result at a flat rate. Check the current rate on the figures sheet before you file — Michigan's rate has moved in recent years.

The Homestead Property Tax Credit — don't miss this

A refundable Michigan credit for residents whose property taxes are high relative to their income. Renters qualify too — a portion of rent is treated as property tax.

Put this on every intake

"Did you rent or own in 2026, and how much did you pay?"

This is the single most commonly missed item for a Detroit client on a modest income, and it's real money. The client will not raise it. You have to.

Detroit — three forms, one question

The question is where did they live:

FormWhoWhat's taxed
5118Lived in Detroit all yearAll income, resident rate
5119Lived outside, worked in DetroitDetroit-earned income only, lower rate
5120Moved in or out during the yearSplit across both periods
The trap

No city tax withheld does not mean no city return is due. A Detroit resident whose employer didn't withhold city tax still owes it — and will owe it with interest if nobody notices.

Try it

Andre lived in Detroit until 31 May 2026, then moved to Southfield. He worked at the same Detroit employer all year, and his W-2 shows Detroit withholding for the whole twelve months. Which form, and what should you be looking for?

Form 5120 — part-year resident

January to May he was a resident, taxed on everything at the resident rate. June to December he was a non-resident working in Detroit, taxed only on Detroit earnings at the lower rate.

His employer kept withholding at the resident rate all year. So Andre has very likely overpaid and is owed a city refund.

The trap: filing 5118 for the whole year because the withholding looks like a full-year resident. Ask when he moved — otherwise he loses money and never knows.

Knowledge check

Five questions. 70% to pass.

1. A client lives in Warren and works downtown. Which Detroit form?

Lives outside, works inside — non-resident.

2. Can a renter claim the Michigan Homestead Property Tax Credit?

Renters qualify — part of the rent is treated as property tax.

3. A Detroit resident's W-2 shows no city withholding. Is a city return due?

Withholding and liability are different things.

4. A client moved out of Detroit in June. Which form?

Moved during the year — part-year.

5. Which intake question catches the Homestead credit?

Ask it of every client, every time.

The software

This module is hands-on. You'll create a client, enter the data, attach the forms, clear every diagnostic, transmit, and retrieve the acknowledgement. You'll do the whole cycle at least three times before Module 8.

When a return rejects

It will happen, often on your busiest day. The common ones:

  • Name or SSN mismatch — usually a typo, sometimes a name change
  • Dependant already claimed — someone else filed first
  • Missing 1095-A — the client has Marketplace coverage and didn't bring the form
  • Prior-year AGI mismatch — an identity check, not a mistake
The sentence you'll need most

"Someone else has already claimed your daughter on their return."

That is a hard thing to hear, and often the first a parent knows of it. Practise saying it calmly. Then explain the rules from Module 3, find out who else may have claimed, and if your client is entitled, we file on paper and the IRS sorts it out.

Don't accuse anyone. Don't guess. Get your trainer if it gets difficult.

The intake interview

Don't just react to whatever paperwork lands on the desk. Ask the same questions every time:

  • Who lived in your home in 2026, and for how many months?
  • Who paid the rent or mortgage and the bills?
  • Did anything change — marriage, separation, a birth, a move, a death?
  • Any income without a form? Cash work, side jobs, apps?
  • Did you rent or own, and how much did you pay?
  • Do you get tips or overtime? Are you 65 or over?
  • Did anyone else claim your children last year?

Seven questions. They take three minutes and they catch nearly everything.

Never promise a refund date

The IRS doesn't guarantee one, and returns claiming EITC or the Additional Child Tax Credit are held by law until later in the season.

Say this instead: "The IRS usually issues most refunds within 21 days of accepting the return, and returns with certain credits are held a bit longer."

Promise a date and you own every day it's late — and the client will call you, not the IRS.

Knowledge check

Five questions. 70% to pass.

1. A client asks exactly when their refund will arrive. What do you say?

Never a specific date.

2. The return rejects because the dependant is already claimed. First step?

Verify entitlement, explain, and file on paper if your client is right.

3. Which question catches unreported side income?

Ask about the money, not the paperwork — clients don't think of app payouts as "a business".

4. A client has Marketplace health coverage but didn't bring the form. What's missing?

Without it the return can't be completed correctly and will reject.

5. You're not confident about a return in front of you. What's the right move?

Escalating is correct professional behaviour, not failure.

Practice Lab

Four complete returns, built from messy piles of paper, the way they actually arrive. Then the final.

By the end you will
  • Complete a full federal, Michigan and Detroit return from raw documents
  • Handle an interview with a client who isn't being clear

The four returns

  • Lab 1 — Single parent, two children, one W-2 plus rideshare income. Detroit resident, renting. Federal, state, city, EITC, Child Tax Credit, Homestead.
  • Lab 2 — Separated client whose spouse left in October, one child, disputed filing status. The right answer is Married Filing Separately and you have to be willing to say so.
  • Lab 3 — Retired veteran, 68. VA disability, military retirement, Social Security and a part-time tipped job. Schedule 1-A applies.
  • Lab 4 — Grandparent raising two grandchildren, moved out of Detroit in July, small cash cleaning income with partial records.

The difficult interview

Your trainer will play a client who wants a bigger refund, is vague about who lived where, and mentions that "the last place just put it through".

You'll be assessed on whether you ask the right questions, write down the answers, and are willing to say no. All three matter.

When to stop and ask

In your first season you do not handle these alone:

  • IRS notices and letters
  • Identity theft
  • Unfiled returns from previous years
  • Anything involving a company or partnership
  • Anything at all you're unsure about

Asking for help is the correct professional response. It is not a mark against you, and nobody here will treat it as one.

Your first fifteen returns

Every new preparer's first fifteen returns are reviewed by an experienced preparer before they're transmitted. Review carries on until two batches in a row come back clean.

This is support, not suspicion. Everyone here went through it.
